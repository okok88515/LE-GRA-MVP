A Candidate-Union K-Means Framework for CQI-Aware Multicast Grouping

Extends the published single-feature k-means grouping method with independently-generated candidate families, selected by exact utility under a resource-block budget.

Abstract

Multicast video delivery groups users so that a shared radio resource serves all members at once, at a quality no better than the group's weakest member. Grouping by Channel Quality Indicator (CQI) alone is a strong baseline but is provably blind to two structural sources of loss: non-linearity in the CQI-to-rate mapping, and per-band frequency-selective profile differences invisible to a single wideband number. This method keeps CQI k-means as its base but poses the grouping problem as a candidate union: independently-cheap partitioning heuristics each propose a grouping for every group count k = 1…K, every proposal is scored by the same exact dynamic-programming resource allocator, and the highest-scoring proposal is kept. Because scoring is exact and shared, adding a family is provably never worse than omitting it — though that guarantee alone says nothing about how much a given family actually contributes, so the method was decided by a full power-set ablation, not by the guarantee alone: every one of the 7 non-empty combinations of three candidate feature families was measured against CQI alone on real OMNeT++/Simu5G physical-layer simulation. Two families — resource-cost and a pairwise exact-utility-regret graph — each drive a real, consistent gain (regret-graph alone: zero losses in 120 confirmatory test cases); a third, switching-state awareness, contributes markedly less by every measure regardless of what it is combined with, and is excluded from the final method on that quantified basis. The final two-family union's dominant mechanism grows stronger, not weaker, when the real-data population size increases from 24 to 40 users.

1 Problem formulation

A base station serves n video-multicast users over R resource blocks (RBs), of which Ravail ≤ R are budgeted for this service in the current allocation cycle. Each user u reports a per-band achievable rate ru,b for band b, derived from its reported CQI. Video is offered at one of six discrete bitrates,

B = {200, 550, 1500, 3000, 5800, 7500} kbps,
(1)

indexed q = 0…5. Users are partitioned into disjoint multicast groups G1, …, GM; because a multicast transmission is limited by its weakest receiver, a group's achievable rate on band b is

rG,b = minu∈G ru,b.
(2)

Given a group's rate profile, the minimum number of RBs needed to clear a target bitrate is computed exactly by greedily consuming the group's own bands from strongest to weakest until the cumulative rate meets the target:

c(G, q) = min { m : ∑i=1m r↓G,i ≥ B[q] },
(3)

where r↓ denotes the group's per-band rates sorted descending; c(G,q) = ∞ if even the full band set cannot clear the target. The grouping problem is: choose a partition and a per-group quality assignment qG ∈ {−1, 0, …, 5} (−1 = unserved) to maximize total utility subject to ∑G c(G, qG) ≤ Ravail.

2 Utility

Each served user contributes a log-bitrate quality-of-experience score normalized to [0,1],

s(q) = ln B[q] − ln B[0] ln B[5] − ln B[0] ,
(4)

penalized by a switching term weighted by β against the user's previous quality tier qprev. A group's contribution at quality q is

v(G, q) = ∑u∈G s(q) − β |q − qprev,u| 5 ,
(5)

or −2|G| if unserved. Overall utility is the per-user mean of the chosen groups' values, and is what every candidate grouping below is ultimately judged by.

3 Exact allocation

For a fixed partition, choosing each group's quality tier is a multiple-choice knapsack: every group is an item class, every quality tier (or unserved) is one mutually-exclusive choice with cost c(G,q) and value v(G,q), and Ravail is the capacity. Solved exactly by dynamic programming over cumulative RB usage:

1state dp[u] = best achievable total value using exactly u RBs 2dp[0] ← 0 3for each group G in partition: 4 for each (quality, cost) feasible for G: 5 dp[u + cost] ← max(dp[u + cost], dp[u] + v(G, quality)) 6return max over u ≤ R_avail of dp[u]

This is the only place a grouping is ever scored, and it is exact — not a proxy, not a greedy heuristic. Every candidate partition considered anywhere in this method is scored by running this same DP.

4 Candidate-union architecture

Finding the utility-maximizing partition directly is a combinatorial set-partition problem. Instead of searching it, the method pools proposals from several cheap, structurally-different heuristics (families), scores every proposal exactly with the DP above, and keeps the best:

  1. Each family f, for every group count k = 1…K, proposes one partition — K proposals per family.
  2. All proposals from all families are pooled and de-duplicated.
  3. Every unique proposal is scored by the exact DP (Section 3).
  4. The highest-scoring proposal is returned.
Proposition. Because every candidate is scored by the same exact function, the best score over a candidate set can only increase, never decrease, when a family is added to the pool. Adding a family is therefore weakly dominant at any single decision instant: it cannot make the chosen grouping worse, only change it if a genuinely better option appears.

This guarantee is local to one decision instant. Across a temporal sequence of decisions, where a chosen grouping changes each user's state (qprev) for the next decision, two methods with different candidate pools can diverge onto different trajectories — so the guarantee does not extend to a whole trajectory's cumulative utility, only to each individual step. Section 7 reports where this matters in practice.

5 The candidate families

Family 1

CQI k-means

One-dimensional k-means on each user's current wideband CQI. The baseline: fast, and correct whenever CQI alone predicts RB-feasibility well.

Family 2

Resource-cost k-means

k-means on a six-dimensional per-user vector cu = ( c(u,0), …, c(u,5) ), computed by Equation 3 as if u were the sole member of its own group. Two users at identical wideband CQI can require very different RB counts if their per-band profile shape differs — this feature is invisible to Family 1 and visible here.

Family 3

Exact-regret spectral graph

Not k-means: a pairwise affinity graph, clustered by spectral embedding. For every user i and every pair (i,j), the best achievable value alone is computed under an exclusive-budget approximation — as if that user or pair had the full RB budget to itself, avoiding an n2-fold repeat of the true shared-budget DP:

regret(i,j) = vsolo(i) + vsolo(j) − vpair(i,j)
(6)

converted to an affinity wij = exp(−regret / scale), scale = the median positive regret. Spectral clustering embeds users via the k smallest eigenvectors of the unnormalized graph Laplacian L = D − W, then k-means the embedding. This family directly measures the true cost of forcing two users together rather than a proxy distance, which matters because the CQI-to-rate mapping is sharply non-linear at the low end: a numerically small CQI gap between two users can hide a large RB-feasibility cliff that neither Family 1 nor Family 2's linear-distance clustering is built to see.

A fourth candidate — switching-state awareness, k-means on the joint coordinate (CQI, previous quality tier) — was also built and tested via the full power-set ablation in Section 7. It contributed markedly less than Families 2 and 3 by every measure, regardless of which other families it was combined with, and is excluded from the final method on that quantified basis rather than by assumption.

6 Final method

The validated method unions all three families and selects by exact DP score:

1function group(scenario, K, beta): 2 candidates ← [] 3 candidates += CQI_kmeans(scenario.cqi, K) 4 candidates += cost_kmeans(resource_cost(scenario), K) 5 candidates += spectral(regret_affinity(scenario, beta), K) 6 return argmax over unique(candidates) of exact_DP_utility(·, scenario, beta)

Group count is swept 1…K within every family (K=3 in the validated configuration); k-means uses 10 random restarts per call. An earlier shipped variant additionally included switching-state awareness; Section 7 reports the full ablation behind removing it.

7 Empirical validation

Evaluated on real OMNeT++ 6.3 / INET 4.6 / Simu5G 1.4 / Veins 5.3 physical-layer simulation with SUMO-generated vehicle mobility (not the synthetic CQI generator) — two gNBs, per-band CQI reports recorded directly from the simulator, real handover and scheduling dynamics.

Power-set ablation: attributing each family's contribution

The union guarantee (Section 4) means a method with more families cannot score below one with fewer, by construction — so "zero losses" alone is never evidence that a given family is pulling real weight. 8 Complexity

Per allocation instant, two computational blocks run: exact DP scoring (once per unique candidate) and Family 3's candidate generation (once, shared across the K group counts it proposes). They do not scale the same way, and the dominant term is not the one an asymptotic-only reading would suggest.

DP allocation: cheap, and cheaper than the formula implies

allocate_and_evaluate is a multiple-choice knapsack: for each of M groups, for each of up to Ravail+1 reachable RB-usage states, for each of |B| = 6 quality options, one value_fn evaluation is combined into the running best. A per-candidate bound of O(M · Ravail · |B|) is the usual way to state this — but value_fn itself costs O(|group|), and does not depend on the RB-usage state it is being folded into, so it is recomputed identically at every one of the up to Ravail+1 states that reach a given group. Summed over all groups (M groups partition all n users), the tighter bound is O(n · Ravail) — a real, if minor, inefficiency: hoisting the value computation out of the RB-usage loop would drop the Ravail factor entirely. Measured on real data, none of this matters in absolute terms:

Where the cost actually lives — and a correction

Family 3 has two sub-steps: building the O(n2) pairwise regret matrix (pairwise_exact_regret_matrix), then embedding via the Laplacian's k smallest eigenvectors (O(n3) for a dense eigendecomposition), once per k = 1…K. An earlier version of this document stated that the eigendecomposition dominates at larger n, reasoning from asymptotic order alone. Direct measurement contradicts it:

Result — the Complexity claim above was wrong

At n=288 the regret-matrix build is 77× slower than the eigendecomposition it was assumed to be dominated by. The reason is implementation, not asymptotics: pairwise_exact_regret_matrix is an unvectorized Python double loop over n²/2 pairs, each a per-pair function call, while np.linalg.eigh is a single vectorized LAPACK call. O(n2) with a large per-operation constant beats O(n3) run by optimized numerical code, at every scale this pipeline currently supports — the crossover where the eigendecomposition's worse order would start to win has not been reached, and would require substantially larger n than tested here.

Implication at MBS scale

MBS is explicitly a many-user regime — hundreds to thousands of receivers per group, not the tens validated so far. Two further real-data points at n=504 and n=816 fit the regret-matrix build's growth cleanly to build(n) ≈ 0.02566 · n2 ms (predicting the n=504 point from the n=816 point alone is off by 0.4%); the eigendecomposition stays two orders of magnitude below it throughout this range and is not the constraint. Inverting the fit gives, for a given re-grouping latency budget, the largest n the current unvectorized implementation can actually support:

Honest limitation

At the cadence the method is actually validated at, the current implementation supports roughly n ≈ 200 — a full order of magnitude short of MBS's thousands-of-users target. Even granting a re-grouping interval generous enough to require its own justification (Section 7's discussion of decoupling grouping cadence from per-instant DP allocation), the ceiling reaches only the low thousands at a 5-minute cadence that is itself unrealistic for most deployments. This was not exposed by the asymptotic analysis alone; it required measuring the actual implementation. Two concrete, unimplemented fixes follow directly from the diagnosis: vectorizing pairwise_exact_regret_matrix with numpy broadcasting in place of the Python double loop (likely a 100–1000× constant-factor reduction on that step alone — roughly the gap between the measured 200-user ceiling and MBS's thousands-of-users target), and replacing the dense eigendecomposition with a sparse/truncated solver (eigsh) over a sparsified k-nearest-neighbor affinity graph rather than the current fully-dense one. Neither has been implemented or measured; at MBS scale, the candidate-union architecture's fallback — dropping Family 3 and keeping the O(n) families, whose per-family ablation (Section 7) quantifies exactly what is given up by doing so — is the honest deployment answer today, not a hypothetical one.

9 Future work: vectorization and kNN sparsification

Section 8 traced Family 3's real cost to an unvectorized O(n2) Python loop, not the eigendecomposition, and quantified the resulting ceiling (≈200 users at the validated 1-second cadence). Two concrete fixes follow directly from that diagnosis — but they buy different things, and only one of them is a free lunch.

Vectorization: same computation, smaller constant

Replacing the double Python loop in pairwise_exact_regret_matrix with numpy broadcasting — computing best_solo_value for every pair at once as vectorized array operations instead of n²/2 individual Python function calls — is the standard fix for this class of slowdown, and typically yields a 100–1000× constant-factor reduction with no change to what is computed: still the exact regret for every pair. Applying that range to the fitted cost model from Section 8 (build(n) ≈ 0.02566 · n2 ms) pushes the 1-second ceiling from n≈200 to roughly n≈2,000–6,000 — covering most of MBS's target range, with the exactness guarantee and every confirmatory result in Section 7 untouched, since nothing about the algorithm itself changes. The honest limit: this raises the wall, it does not remove it. The computation is still O(n2); at a large enough n the same ceiling reappears, just further out. This is the low-risk, high-value first step, and the one worth building before the one below it.

kNN sparsification: a different algorithm, needing its own validation

Reaching an asymptotically better order requires computing exact regret for fewer than all n²/2 pairs in the first place. The standard approach: use a cheap proxy metric — the same CQI or resource-cost feature vectors Families 1 and 2 already compute — to find each user's k nearest neighbors via a KD-tree or ball-tree (build cost O(n log n), query cost O(n log n) total), then compute the true exact regret only for those O(n·k) candidate pairs; every pair outside a user's candidate neighborhood is assigned affinity zero rather than evaluated. Paired with a sparse graph, the eigendecomposition can also move from dense np.linalg.eigh to an iterative solver (eigsh, Lanczos-based), each iteration costing O(nnz) = O(n·k) on the now-sparse Laplacian.

This is not just a faster version of the same thing

Restricting exact-regret evaluation to a proxy-metric neighborhood assumes pairs the proxy metric calls "far apart" also have negligible true regret. Section 5 is a direct warning against exactly this assumption: two users at identical wideband CQI can still have very different per-band RB-cost profiles, which is the whole reason Family 2 and the exact-regret formulation exist rather than relying on CQI distance alone. A kNN graph built on a CQI-only or resource-cost-only proxy could systematically miss the very outlier pairs the exact formulation was built to catch. This is not a hypothetical caveat to note in passing — it is a real risk that has not been measured, and closing this section's fix would need its own confirmatory ablation, run the same way as Section 7, comparing the sparsified-approximate version against the current exact-all-pairs one before it is trusted as a drop-in replacement. Similarly, the iterative eigensolver's near-O(n log n) behavior depends on the graph's spectral gap and is an empirical, typical-case claim from the sparse spectral clustering literature, not a worst-case guarantee.

The two fixes are complementary, not alternatives, and in that order: vectorization first, because it is low-risk (same algorithm, same guarantees) and already closes most of the gap to MBS scale; sparsification second, because it is what actually removes the asymptotic ceiling rather than just moving it, but changes what is computed and cannot be trusted at MBS scale without its own confirmatory pass. Neither has been implemented as of this document.

§ Summary

The published single-feature CQI k-means method extends cleanly to a candidate union — CQI, resource-cost, and pairwise exact-utility regret — united by an exact DP evaluator that makes every addition provably safe at the decision level. A third candidate, switching-state awareness, was built and measured through the same full power-set ablation and excluded on quantified grounds: it contributed markedly less than the other two in every combination, regardless of dispersion or load. On real physical-layer simulation, confirmatory testing shows the kept two-family method strictly dominates CQI alone, the resource-cost family leads at moderate channel dispersion, the regret-graph family overtakes it and grows further ahead as dispersion and load both tighten, and its contribution keeps growing as the real-data population scales up. That contribution is not yet free: measured directly rather than assumed from asymptotic order alone, Family 3's candidate generation is the real cost bottleneck of the method, and at MBS's true target scale of hundreds to thousands of users, the current unvectorized implementation is not viable at any usable re-grouping cadence (Section 8). The architecture's fallback — drop Family 3, keep the two O(n) families — is a real deployment option today with its cost precisely quantified (Section 7); closing the gap without giving up the mechanism has a concrete, staged path — vectorization first, kNN sparsification second — laid out in Section 9, neither step of which has been built yet.
